What is pharmacodynamics?

Pharmacodynamics (PD) is the study of what a drug does to the body: its actions, how they happen (mechanism) and how the effect changes with dose. Pharmacokinetics is the opposite: what the body does to the drug.

  • A drug changes a function that already exists. It cannot give a cell a brand-new function.
  • Effect depends on the drug reaching its target in enough amount (PK) and on the target itself (PD).
  • Most drugs act by binding a receptor. Some act without a receptor (physical, chemical, enzyme, channel or carrier).

1. Principles and mechanisms of drug action

Types of drug action

Type of actionMeaningExample
StimulationRaises the activity of a cell or organAdrenaline on the heart

ReplacementGives a substance the body lacksInsulin in diabetes, thyroxine in hypothyroidism, iron in anaemia

MechanismHow the drug worksExamples

2. Receptors: theories, classification and regulation

Receptor and key terms

A receptor is a protein (rarely another molecule) in the cell membrane, cytoplasm or nucleus that recognises a specific drug or body chemical and starts a response. The drug or messenger that binds is called a ligand.

TermMeaningExample
AffinityHow strongly a drug sticks to its receptorShown by all agonists and antagonists
Intrinsic activity (efficacy)Ability of the bound drug to start a response. Full agonist = 1, antagonist = 0Agonist 1, partial agonist between 0 and 1
Full agonistBinds and gives the maximum responseMorphine at mu receptors, adrenaline
Partial agonistBinds but gives only a smaller response, even at full occupancyBuprenorphine, pindolol
Inverse agonistBinds and lowers the receptor’s own resting activity (opposite effect)Many H1 blockers such as cetirizine, beta-carbolines at GABA-A
AntagonistBinds but gives no response and blocks the agonistAtropine, propranolol, naloxone, flumazenil

Spare receptors

Some tissues have more receptors than are needed for the maximum response. A full effect is reached when only a small part of the receptors is occupied. The rest are the spare receptors (receptor reserve).

  • The tissue is very sensitive: a small dose of agonist gives a full effect (ED50 is far lower than the dose that fills half the receptors).
  • A competitive antagonist must block many receptors before the response falls, so higher doses of antagonist are needed.
  • Example: heart muscle and smooth muscle have a large reserve for adrenaline and acetylcholine.

Receptor theories

TheoryIdea
Occupation theory (Clark, later Ariens and Stephenson)Effect is proportional to the number of receptors occupied. Ariens added intrinsic activity, Stephenson added efficacy to explain partial agonists
Rate theory (Paton)Effect depends on the rate at which the drug binds and leaves the receptor, not on how many are occupied. Fast on and off = agonist. Slow off = antagonist
Induced fit theory (Koshland)The agonist changes the shape of the receptor as it binds, and this starts the response. An antagonist binds without this shape change
Two-state (allosteric) modelThe receptor flips between inactive (R) and active (R*) forms. Agonists prefer R*, antagonists bind both equally, inverse agonists prefer R

Memory aid: Clark = occupancy. Paton = rate. Koshland = induced fit (the receptor changes shape like a glove around a hand).

Exam tip Occupation theory fails for partial agonists, so Ariens added intrinsic activity and Stephenson added efficacy. Rate theory explains why some drugs with high affinity still do not activate.

Classification of receptors

Receptor familySpeedExamplesKey point
Ligand-gated ion channel (ionotropic)MillisecondsNicotinic (Nm, Nn), GABA-A, glycine, NMDA, 5-HT3Binding opens the channel. Na+/Ca2+ in = excitation. Cl- in = inhibition
G-protein coupled (metabotropic)SecondsMuscarinic, adrenergic, dopamine, opioid, H1, H2, most 5-HTSeven transmembrane helices, uses G proteins and second messengers
Enzyme-linked (kinase)MinutesInsulin, EGF, PDGF. ANP (guanylyl cyclase)Has an enzyme part inside the cell, usually tyrosine kinase

Regulation of receptors

Receptor number and sensitivity are not fixed. The cell adjusts them to the signal it receives.

ChangeWhat happensExample
Down-regulation (desensitisation)Long or repeated agonist exposure: receptors get phosphorylated, uncoupled and taken inside the cell, so fewer are on the surfaceTolerance to opioids, falling response to salbutamol with heavy use
Up-regulation (supersensitivity)Long exposure to an antagonist or loss of nerve supply: more receptors appearRebound hypertension or angina after sudden stopping of propranolol or clonidine

3. Drug-receptor interactions and signal transduction

Drug-receptor interactions

The drug (D) and receptor (R) join and part again: D + R ↔ DR complex → response. Binding follows the law of mass action. Kd is the drug concentration at which half the receptors are occupied, so a low Kd means high affinity.

BondStrengthEffect on the drug action
CovalentStrongestAlmost irreversible and long-lasting. Examples: phenoxybenzamine on alpha receptors, aspirin on COX, omeprazole on the proton pump
Ionic (electrostatic)MediumReversible. Charged groups of the drug attract opposite charges on the receptor, as with acetylcholine
Hydrogen bondWeakReversible. Gives the drug its fit and selectivity
HydrophobicWeakReversible. Fat-loving parts of the drug sit in a fatty pocket of the receptor
Van der WaalsWeakestReversible. Acts only when drug and receptor are very close

Most drugs use weak bonds, so their effect wears off as the drug leaves. A covalent bond ends only when the body makes new receptor or enzyme.

Exam tip More agonist can overcome a reversible competitive antagonist but not an irreversible (covalent) one such as phenoxybenzamine.

G-protein coupled receptors (GPCR)

A GPCR has seven transmembrane helices. When the agonist binds, the receptor activates a G protein (alpha, beta, gamma subunits). The alpha subunit swaps GDP for GTP and goes on to work on an effector. The signal ends when the alpha subunit splits GTP back to GDP.

G proteinEffector and messengerReceptor examplesResult
GsStimulates adenylyl cyclase, cAMP up, PKA onBeta1, beta2, D1, H2, V2Heart rate and force up, bronchi and vessels relax, glycogenolysis, lipolysis
GiInhibits adenylyl cyclase, cAMP down. Opens K+ channelsM2, M4, alpha2, D2, opioid, 5-HT1Heart slows, neuron less excitable, less transmitter release
GqActivates phospholipase C, makes IP3 and DAGM1, M3, alpha1, H1, 5-HT2, V1, AT1IP3 releases Ca2+ from stores, DAG activates PKC. Smooth muscle contracts, glands secrete
  1. Agonist binds the GPCR.
  2. G protein exchanges GDP for GTP and the alpha subunit separates.
  3. The alpha subunit (or beta-gamma) acts on an effector: adenylyl cyclase, phospholipase C or an ion channel.
  4. Second messenger forms: cAMP or IP3 and DAG.
  5. Protein kinases are switched on (PKA by cAMP, PKC by DAG) and the cell responds.

Exam tip Beta receptors act through Gs and cAMP. Alpha-1, M1, M3 and H1 act through Gq and IP3/DAG. Alpha-2, M2 and opioid receptors act through Gi and lower cAMP. Cholera toxin keeps Gs switched on. Pertussis toxin blocks Gi.

Ligand-gated ion channel receptors

  • The receptor is itself a channel made of several subunits. Binding of the agonist opens the pore within milliseconds.
  • Excitatory: nicotinic (Na+ in), NMDA (Ca2+ and Na+ in), 5-HT3.
  • Inhibitory: GABA-A (Cl- in) and glycine. Benzodiazepines and barbiturates act on GABA-A.

Enzyme-linked (transmembrane kinase) receptors

  1. The receptor has one transmembrane helix, an outside binding site and an inside enzyme domain (usually tyrosine kinase).
  2. Agonist binding pairs two receptors (dimerisation).
  3. The receptors phosphorylate each other on tyrosine (autophosphorylation).
  4. Adapter proteins bind the phosphorylated sites and start cascades (for example the MAP kinase and PI3K pathways).
  5. Result: growth, metabolism and gene expression changes, over minutes to hours.

Examples: insulin, EGF, PDGF, VEGF. ANP uses the same plan, but its enzyme is guanylyl cyclase (makes cGMP).

JAK-STAT receptors (cytokine receptors)

These receptors have no enzyme of their own. Janus kinases (JAK) are attached to the receptor’s inner tail.

  1. Ligand binding brings receptor chains together.
  2. JAKs phosphorylate each other and the receptor.
  3. STAT proteins dock on the phosphates and are phosphorylated.
  4. STATs pair up (dimerise), move into the nucleus and switch on genes.

Examples: growth hormone, prolactin, erythropoietin, interferons, interleukins. Tofacitinib is a JAK inhibitor used in rheumatoid arthritis.

Receptors that regulate transcription (nuclear receptors)

  • The ligand is lipid soluble, crosses the cell membrane and binds a receptor in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
  • The drug-receptor complex binds a hormone response element on DNA and increases or decreases transcription of particular genes.
  • New protein is made, so the effect starts slowly (hours) and lasts long (days).
  • Examples: corticosteroids, oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone, thyroid hormone, vitamin D, retinoids.

Exam tip If a question asks why steroids act slowly, the answer is gene transcription and new protein synthesis. This is the only family that works through DNA.

A bigger dose gives a bigger effect, up to a ceiling. If the dose is plotted on a log scale, the curve turns into a neat S shape (sigmoid). There are two kinds of curve.

ExampleBumetanide is more potent than furosemideFurosemide (high ceiling) has more efficacy than thiazides
  • ED50: dose that gives 50 percent of the maximum effect (graded) or an effect in 50 percent of people (quantal).
  • Competitive antagonist shifts the agonist curve to the right with the same Emax (lower apparent potency).
  • Non-competitive antagonist lowers Emax.
  • Partial agonist reaches a lower Emax than a full agonist.

Therapeutic index (TI)

TI tells how safe a drug is. It compares the dose that harms with the dose that helps.

  • TI = LD50 / ED50 in animals. In patients: TD50 / ED50 (toxic dose in 50 percent / effective dose in 50 percent).
  • A large TI means a wide safety margin (penicillin). A small TI means narrow margin, so dose and blood levels must be watched.
  • Narrow TI examples: digoxin, lithium, warfarin, phenytoin, theophylline, aminoglycosides.
  • Certain safety factor = LD1 / ED99 (a stricter measure). The therapeutic window is the range of plasma levels between the minimum effective level and the level where toxicity starts.

Memory aid: Narrow TI list: Digoxin, Lithium, Warfarin, Phenytoin, Theophylline. Think “Dig Little Wars, Please Test”.

Exam tip A drug with high potency is not always better. Clinically, efficacy and TI matter more. Also remember: LD50 is only used in animals.

bAdverse drug reactionsTypes A to F, allergy, special reactions, prevention

Definition

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful and unintended response to a drug given at the normal dose for prevention, diagnosis or treatment (WHO definition). An overdose or a wrong use is not counted as an ADR; it is poisoning or a medication error.

Related terms

  • Side effect: an unwanted but predictable effect at normal dose, which is part of the drug’s pharmacology (dry mouth with atropine). Some are even useful.
  • Toxic effect: harm caused by too high a dose or too long a use.
  • Adverse event: any bad event after taking a drug, whether the drug caused it or not.
  • Serious ADR: causes death, hospital stay, disability, birth defect or is life-threatening.

Prevention and management

  • Take a proper drug and allergy history before prescribing.

  • Record the reaction and report it to the pharmacovigilance system (see topic d).

cDrug interactionsPharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic, with examples

Definition

A drug interaction happens when the effect of one drug is changed by another drug, food, drink or herb taken at the same time. The result can be harmful (more toxicity, treatment failure) or useful (levodopa with carbidopa).

  • Two kinds: pharmacokinetic (the level of the drug changes) and pharmacodynamic (the effect changes at the same level).
  • Risk is highest with narrow-TI drugs (warfarin, digoxin, lithium, phenytoin), with many drugs together (polypharmacy), and in the elderly.

  • Inducer: other drug level falls, effect weaker. Slow onset (days to weeks), also slow to wear off.
  • Inhibitor: other drug level rises, toxicity risk. Quick onset (1 to 3 days).

  • Protein-binding displacement matters mostly for drugs that are highly bound, have a small volume of distribution and a narrow TI. The effect is often short-lived because the freed drug is cleared faster.

Exam tip Warfarin with aspirin raises the bleeding risk. The main reason is that aspirin affects platelets and the stomach lining, plus some displacement. The main textbook example of a protein-binding displacement is warfarin with sulfonamides or phenylbutazone.

Pharmacovigilance

Pharmacovigilance (PV) is the science and activity of detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing adverse effects and other drug-related problems (WHO definition).

  • Why: clinical trials involve limited numbers, short time and selected patients. Rare, delayed and special-group ADRs show up only after wide use.
  • Spontaneous (voluntary) reporting by doctors, pharmacists, nurses and patients is the basic method. Other methods: cohort studies, case-control studies, prescription event monitoring.
  • International: WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring, with the Uppsala Monitoring Centre (Sweden) as the coordinating centre.
  • India: Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI), started in 2010 and run by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC), Ghaziabad. ADRs are reported on a suspected ADR form, by the helpline or the ADR PvPI app.
  • Causality check: WHO-UMC categories (certain, probable, possible, unlikely) or the Naranjo scale.
  • Outcome: a signal can lead to a label change, a warning, restriction or withdrawal of the drug.

Exam tip Know the difference: Phase IV is a study done after approval, and pharmacovigilance is the continuing system for monitoring drug safety. The two work together.

aOrganization and function of ANSSympathetic vs parasympathetic, with organ effects

What is the ANS?

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is the part of the nervous system that works without our will. It controls smooth muscle, heart muscle and glands. It keeps the internal environment steady (heart rate, BP, digestion, pupil size, body temperature).

Basic plan

  • Two divisions: sympathetic and parasympathetic. The enteric nervous system (gut wall network) is sometimes counted as a third.
  • The efferent path has two neurons: preganglionic neuron, then a ganglion, then postganglionic neuron, then the effector organ.
  • Preganglionic fibres are myelinated (B type). Postganglionic fibres are non-myelinated (C type).
  • Exception: the adrenal medulla is supplied directly by preganglionic sympathetic fibres. It behaves like a modified ganglion and releases adrenaline into blood.

bNeurohumoral transmission, co-transmission and classification of neurotransmittersSteps, ACh and noradrenaline cycles, receptors

Definition

Neurohumoral transmission means passing a nerve impulse across a synapse or a nerve-to-organ junction with the help of a chemical messenger (neurotransmitter).

Steps

  1. Impulse travels down the axon (axonal conduction).
  2. At the terminal, Ca2+ enters and vesicles release the transmitter into the gap.
  3. Transmitter binds to receptors on the next cell.
  4. Response occurs (excitation or inhibition of the postjunctional cell).
  5. Action is ended by enzymes, re-uptake or diffusion away.

Co-transmission

One nerve ending can release more than one transmitter. The extra ones are called co-transmitters. They fine-tune the main response.

  • Sympathetic nerve: noradrenaline + ATP + neuropeptide Y.
  • Parasympathetic nerve: acetylcholine + VIP.
  • Sensory nerve: glutamate + substance P.

Classification of neurotransmitters

Which nerves are cholinergic?

  • All preganglionic fibres (sympathetic and parasympathetic).
  • All postganglionic parasympathetic fibres.
  • Postganglionic sympathetic fibres to sweat glands.
  • Somatic motor nerves to skeletal muscle.
  • Adrenergic: most other postganglionic sympathetic fibres.

Acetylcholine cycle

  1. Synthesis: choline + acetyl-CoA, by choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)
  2. Storage in vesicles
  3. Release by Ca2+ triggered exocytosis (blocked by botulinum toxin)
  4. Action on muscarinic or nicotinic receptors
  5. Breakdown by acetylcholinesterase into choline + acetate. Choline is taken back into the nerve.

Exam tip Hemicholinium blocks choline uptake. Botulinum toxin blocks ACh release. Both are classic one-line questions.

Noradrenaline cycle

  1. Tyrosine to DOPA by tyrosine hydroxylase (rate-limiting step)
  2. DOPA to dopamine by DOPA decarboxylase
  3. Dopamine enters the vesicle (blocked by reserpine)
  4. Dopamine to noradrenaline by dopamine β-hydroxylase
  5. Release by Ca2+ exocytosis
  6. Action on α or β receptors
  7. Termination: mainly re-uptake into the nerve (uptake-1, blocked by cocaine and tricyclic antidepressants). Then breakdown by MAO and COMT into VMA.

In the adrenal medulla, noradrenaline is further changed into adrenaline by PNMT.

cParasympathomimetics, parasympatholytics, sympathomimetics, sympatholyticsFour drug groups: classes, MOA, uses, adverse effects

1. Parasympathomimetics (cholinergic drugs)

Drugs that copy or increase the action of acetylcholine.

Classification

Mechanism of action

  • Direct: bind to and activate muscarinic (or nicotinic) receptors.
  • Indirect: block acetylcholinesterase, so ACh stays longer and builds up.

Effects

Pupil constricts, heart slows, bronchi narrow, glands secrete more, gut and bladder contract more.

Uses

  • Pilocarpine: glaucoma, dry mouth.
  • Bethanechol: urinary retention, paralytic ileus.
  • Neostigmine: myasthenia gravis, reversal of non-depolarising blockers, ileus, urinary retention.
  • Physostigmine: atropine poisoning (it crosses the blood-brain barrier).
  • Edrophonium: diagnosis of myasthenia gravis (short acting).
  • Donepezil, rivastigmine: Alzheimer's disease.

Adverse effects

Cholinergic excess: DUMBELS = Diarrhoea, Urination, Miosis, Bradycardia and Bronchospasm, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation and Sweating.

Contraindications

Bronchial asthma, peptic ulcer, intestinal or urinary obstruction, bradycardia or heart block.

Exam tip Organophosphate poisoning: give atropine (blocks muscarinic effects) and pralidoxime (reactivates the enzyme, works best if given early). Neostigmine is quaternary and stays outside the brain. Physostigmine is tertiary and enters the brain.

Palpitations, tachycardia, arrhythmia, high BP, tremor, anxiety, headache. Noradrenaline leaking out of the vein can damage tissue.

Contraindications

Hypertension, hyperthyroidism, arrhythmias, coronary disease. Take care with MAO inhibitors and halothane.

Exam tip Dale's vasomotor reversal: if α receptors are blocked first, adrenaline lowers BP instead of raising it (only β2 vasodilation is left). Noradrenaline raises BP and causes reflex bradycardia.

4. Sympatholytics (adrenergic antagonists and blockers)

Drugs that block the effect of sympathetic stimulation.

Classification

TypeDrugs
α blockers, non-selectivePhenoxybenzamine (irreversible), phentolamine (reversible)
α1 blockersPrazosin, doxazosin, terazosin, tamsulosin
α2 blockerYohimbine
β blockers, non-selectivePropranolol, nadolol, timolol, sotalol
β1 selectiveAtenolol, metoprolol, bisoprolol, esmolol
α + β blockersLabetalol, carvedilol
Adrenergic neuron blockers and central agentsReserpine, guanethidine, methyldopa, clonidine

Uses

  • Phenoxybenzamine: pheochromocytoma.
  • Prazosin, doxazosin: hypertension, BPH. Tamsulosin: BPH.
  • Propranolol: hypertension, angina, arrhythmia, migraine prevention, thyrotoxicosis symptoms, tremor.
  • Timolol: glaucoma (eye drops).
  • Labetalol, carvedilol: hypertension, heart failure (carvedilol).

Adverse effects

  • α blockers: postural hypotension (first dose), reflex tachycardia, blocked nose.
  • β blockers: bradycardia, bronchospasm, tiredness, cold hands and feet, sleep disturbance, can hide signs of low blood sugar. Do not stop suddenly (rebound).

Contraindications of β blockers

Asthma and COPD, heart block, severe bradycardia, uncontrolled heart failure.

Memory aid: Drug names ending -olol are β blockers. Ending -osin are α1 blockers. β1 selective = BEAM: Bisoprolol, Esmolol, Atenolol, Metoprolol.

Exam tip Phenoxybenzamine binds covalently, so its block is long lasting. Phentolamine is reversible and short acting.

dNeuromuscular blocking agents and skeletal muscle relaxants (peripheral)Non-depolarising vs depolarising, dantrolene

Skeletal muscle relaxants (SMR)

Drugs that reduce skeletal muscle tone or contraction. They either act at the nerve-muscle junction (peripheral) or in the brain and spinal cord (central).

GroupDrugs
Peripheral: non-depolarising (competitive) NMBsLong: pancuronium, tubocurarine. Intermediate: atracurium, cisatracurium, vecuronium, rocuronium. Short: mivacurium
Peripheral: depolarising NMBsSuccinylcholine (suxamethonium), decamethonium
Peripheral: direct acting on muscleDantrolene
Central (for completeness)Baclofen, diazepam, tizanidine, methocarbamol, chlorzoxazone, cyclobenzaprine

Non-depolarising blockers (curare type)

  • MOA: compete with ACh at the Nm receptor on the motor end plate. They do not activate the receptor, so the muscle cannot contract. Result: flaccid paralysis.
  • Order of paralysis: small fast muscles first (eyes, fingers), then limbs, trunk, intercostals and diaphragm last. Recovery is in the reverse order.
  • Reversal: by neostigmine (more ACh wins the competition), given with atropine or glycopyrrolate. Sugammadex reverses rocuronium and vecuronium.
DrugPoints to remember
TubocurarineReleases histamine, causes hypotension and bronchospasm. Blocks ganglia. Rarely used now
PancuroniumBlocks vagus, so tachycardia. Long acting
Atracurium, cisatracuriumBroken down spontaneously in plasma (Hofmann elimination), so safe in liver or kidney failure. Atracurium may release histamine, cisatracurium much less
VecuroniumFew heart effects
RocuroniumFastest onset of this group

Depolarising blocker: succinylcholine

  • MOA: acts like ACh on Nm receptors but is not broken down quickly at the junction. The end plate stays depolarised, so it cannot fire again. First brief twitching (fasciculations), then paralysis.
  • Broken down by plasma pseudocholinesterase. Very short acting (a few minutes).
  • Neostigmine does not reverse it. It can make the block worse.

Uses of neuromuscular blockers

Muscle relaxation during surgery, helping tracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, ECT (to prevent injury from convulsions). They give no pain relief and no sleep, so the patient must be under anaesthesia.

Adverse effects

  • Succinylcholine: high blood K+, muscle pains after surgery, raised eye and stomach pressure, bradycardia, malignant hyperthermia, long paralysis in pseudocholinesterase deficiency.
  • Non-depolarising: histamine release, hypotension, prolonged paralysis with aminoglycosides or magnesium.

Contraindications of succinylcholine

Burns, crush injury, nerve or muscle disease (risk of dangerous hyperkalaemia), raised eye pressure, family history of malignant hyperthermia.

Directly acting: dantrolene

Acts inside the muscle fibre. Blocks the ryanodine receptor and stops Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, so the muscle contracts less. Use: malignant hyperthermia (drug of choice), spasticity. Adverse effects: liver toxicity, weakness, drowsiness.

Centrally acting relaxants in brief

They reduce nerve activity in the brain or spinal cord, mostly by boosting GABA (baclofen is a GABA-B agonist, diazepam enhances GABA-A) or by α2 action (tizanidine). Used in muscle spasm, spasticity and back pain. Common adverse effects: drowsiness, dizziness, weakness.

Exam tip "Non-depolarising block is reversed by neostigmine, depolarising block is not" is a favourite short question. Also remember: dantrolene = malignant hyperthermia.

eLocal anesthetic agentsClassification, MOA, uses, toxicity

Definition

Local anaesthetics (LAs) temporarily block nerve conduction in one area of the body. Pain sensation is lost and the patient stays conscious.

Chemistry

Three parts: an aromatic ring (fat-loving), a middle link (ester or amide) and an amine group (water-loving).

TypeExamplesPoints
EsterProcaine, chloroprocaine, tetracaine, cocaine, benzocaineBroken down by plasma esterases. Allergy is more common (PABA)
AmideLidocaine, prilocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, dibucaineBroken down in the liver. Allergy is rare

Classification

GroupDrugs
Injectable: low potency, short durationProcaine, chloroprocaine
Injectable: intermediateLidocaine, prilocaine
Injectable: high potency, long durationTetracaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, dibucaine
Surface, solubleCocaine, lidocaine, tetracaine, proparacaine
Surface, insolubleBenzocaine, butamben (butylaminobenzoate), oxethazaine

Mechanism of action

  1. LAs are weak bases. The un-ionised form crosses the nerve membrane.
  2. Inside the nerve, the ionised form binds to the voltage-gated Na+ channel from the inside.
  3. Na+ cannot enter, so no depolarisation and no action potential.
  4. Pain signals do not reach the brain. The effect wears off as the drug is cleared.

Small fibres are blocked first: pain, then temperature, touch, pressure and last motor power. In infected (acidic) tissue less un-ionised drug is available, so LAs work poorly.

Adrenaline is added to the injection. It narrows vessels, so the LA stays longer, acts longer and is less toxic. Do not use it in fingers, toes, ear or penis (risk of poor blood supply).

Techniques

Surface (topical), infiltration, nerve block, spinal, epidural, IV regional block.

Individual drugs

  • Lidocaine: most used, all techniques. Also used for ventricular arrhythmias.
  • Bupivacaine: long acting, spinal and epidural. Can be toxic to the heart.
  • Ropivacaine: like bupivacaine but safer for the heart.
  • Procaine: short acting, infiltration.
  • Tetracaine: spinal and eye drops.
  • Prilocaine: can cause methaemoglobinaemia. Used with lidocaine in EMLA cream.
  • Cocaine: only LA that also narrows vessels. Topical in ENT. Abuse risk.
  • Benzocaine: topical, on skin and mucous membranes.

Adverse effects

  • Local: irritation, tissue damage.
  • Allergy (mainly esters).
  • Systemic toxicity, CNS: restlessness, tremor, convulsions, then depression.
  • Systemic toxicity, heart: low BP, bradycardia, arrhythmia.
  • Spinal block: headache, low BP.

Contraindications and cautions

Known allergy, infection at the injection site, heart block (bupivacaine), severe heart disease (with adrenaline).

Memory aid: Amide names have an i before "caine": lidocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, prilocaine, dibucaine. Esters do not: procaine, tetracaine, cocaine, benzocaine.

Exam tip Mechanism = block of voltage-gated Na+ channels. Bupivacaine = cardiotoxic. Prilocaine = methaemoglobinaemia. Cocaine = vasoconstrictor LA.

fDrugs used in myasthenia gravis and glaucomaDisease basics, drug classes, MOA, adverse effects

Myasthenia gravis (MG)

A long-term autoimmune disease. The body makes antibodies against Nm receptors at the neuromuscular junction. Fewer working receptors means weak muscles that tire quickly with use and recover with rest.

  1. Immune system makes antibodies against the body's own receptors
  2. Antibodies block or destroy Nm receptors
  3. ACh cannot pass the signal to the muscle properly
  4. Weakness and fatigue: drooping eyelids, double vision, slurred speech, trouble swallowing, limb weakness

Thymus gland problems (such as a thymoma) are often linked.

Drug treatment

ClassDrugs and MOA
AnticholinesterasesPyridostigmine (first choice, oral), neostigmine. They block AChE, so ACh stays longer at the junction and muscle power improves. Edrophonium is used for diagnosis
CorticosteroidsPrednisolone. Lower the immune attack and antibody production
Other immunosuppressantsAzathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, cyclophosphamide, rituximab. Reduce antibody production
Other measuresThymectomy, plasma exchange, IV immunoglobulin (in crisis)

Adverse effects

  • Anticholinesterases: cramps, diarrhoea, sweating, salivation, bradycardia. Too much causes cholinergic crisis. Atropine or glycopyrrolate can control the muscarinic effects.
  • Steroids: high blood sugar, weight gain, bone thinning, high BP, infection.
  • Azathioprine: low blood counts, liver toxicity, infection.

Contraindications of anticholinesterases: intestinal or urinary obstruction, asthma.

Exam tip Edrophonium test: weakness improves in myasthenic crisis (too little ACh action) and gets worse in cholinergic crisis (too much). Aminoglycosides, magnesium and neuromuscular blockers can worsen MG.

Glaucoma

A group of eye diseases where raised eye pressure (intraocular pressure, IOP) damages the optic nerve. It can lead to vision loss. Open-angle glaucoma is slow and painless. Angle-closure glaucoma is sudden and painful: it is an emergency.

  1. Aqueous humour drains poorly (or is made too much)
  2. It builds up and IOP rises
  3. Optic nerve fibres get compressed and lose blood flow
  4. Nerve damage and vision loss

Aqueous humour is made by the ciliary body and drains through the trabecular meshwork and the uveoscleral route. Every glaucoma drug does one of two things: makes less fluid or drains more fluid.

Drugs

ClassDrugsMOA and adverse effects
Prostaglandin analoguesLatanoprost, travoprost, bimatoprostMore drainage (uveoscleral). Iris darkening, eyelash growth, red eye
β blockersTimolol, betaxolol, carteolol, levobunololLess fluid made. Bradycardia and bronchospasm can occur (avoid in asthma)
α2 agonistsBrimonidine, apraclonidineLess made and more drained. Dry mouth, drowsiness, eye allergy
Adrenaline prodrugDipivefrineMore drainage. Eye irritation
Carbonic anhydrase inhibitorsAcetazolamide, dorzolamide, brinzolamideLess fluid made. Tingling, metallic taste, kidney stones, acidosis. Sulfonamide allergy
Miotics (cholinergic)Pilocarpine, carbachol, physostigminePupil and ciliary muscle contract, drainage channels open. Blurred or dim vision, brow ache, myopia
Osmotic agents (emergency)Mannitol, glycerolPull fluid out of the eye. Used in acute angle-closure

Memory aid: Less made = B-blockers, CAIs, Alpha-2 agonists (BCA). More drained = Prostaglandins, Miotics (PM).

Exam tip Pilocarpine makes the pupil small and opens the drainage angle. Atropine-like drugs widen the pupil and can trigger an attack in narrow-angle glaucoma, so they are avoided.
